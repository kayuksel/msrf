Beyond Parameter Fitting: Search-First ML via Open-Ended Evolution of Computation
Kamer Ali Yuksel
Abstract
Modern machine learning is dominated by a single organizing principle: define a parameterized model and optimize its weights. This paradigm has been extraordinarily successful, but it has also become too narrow. In many important settings, the true bottleneck is not the inability to fit another large parameter vector, but the inability to discover what should be computed from the data in the first place. Predictive success often depends on the right statistics, transformations, invariances, interaction structures, spectral summaries, change-point signals, or domain-specific nonlinear compositions. Yet contemporary ML frameworks typically force these computations into one of two roles: either they are hand-engineered by humans, or they are left to emerge implicitly inside large differentiable models through gradient descent.
We argue for a complementary paradigm: search-first machine learning, in which learning is performed primarily through structured search over reusable computations, while downstream prediction is handled by sparse, closed-form, or otherwise minimal-parameter selection. In this view, the principal object of learning is not a monolithic trainable network, but an evolving computational graph whose nodes represent candidate functions, transformations, and compositional motifs. Large language models (LLMs) act as search operators over this space, proposing, revising, and recombining computational hypotheses. Lightweight predictors such as ridge regression then identify which evolved computations matter, while their internal diagnostics are fed back into the search process as evidence for future discovery.
We present EvoForest as a concrete instance of this paradigm. EvoForest represents a population of candidate computations inside a shared directed acyclic graph (DAG), where nodes contain multiple alternative implementations, callable higher-order function families, and optionally persistent gradient-trained parameters. Its graph structure enables compositional feature synthesis and computational sharing; its evaluation layer separates structural search from feature selection; and its diagnostic loop transforms model internals into actionable evolutionary signals. Preliminary results on structural regime detection show that the system autonomously rediscovers classical statistical machinery, composes it into deeper learnable structures, and exhibits an emergent simplification-through-depth effect in which fewer but more structured features outperform broader shallow ensembles.
Our claim is broader than any one implementation. We argue that machine learning should expand beyond parameter-centric optimization toward a broader class of systems in which useful computations are explicitly searched, reused, selected, and refined. This perspective unifies and extends ideas from feature engineering, genetic programming, neural architecture search, random features, mixture-of-experts, and LLM-guided program synthesis. We outline a research agenda for evolutionary computation graphs, parameter-light predictors, diagnostic-guided search, and hybrid neuro-symbolic learning systems in which symbolic creativity and continuous refinement occupy complementary roles.
1. Introduction
The dominant recipe in modern machine learning is familiar: specify a function class, choose a loss, and fit parameters. Whether the model is linear, kernel-based, recurrent, convolutional, or transformer-based, the organizing principle is the same. Learning is treated primarily as optimization over a parameter space. This paradigm has delivered extraordinary progress. But it has also shaped the field’s imagination so thoroughly that it is often treated as synonymous with learning itself. We argue that this identification is too narrow. In many real-world tasks, the central challenge is not parameter fitting. It is computational discovery. Success depends on finding the right transformations to compute from the data: robust change statistics, shape descriptors, asymmetric interactions, temporal summaries, spectral measures, gating structures, similarity kernels, or domain-specific invariants. These are not merely details layered on top of a model. They are often the real substance of the predictive problem. Yet most of machine learning either asks humans to engineer them manually or asks large parameterized systems to induce them implicitly through optimization. This is especially visible in tabular learning, time-series forecasting, scientific detection, finance, healthcare, and industrial monitoring. In such domains, carefully designed features still compete strongly with end-to-end representation learning, particularly when data are limited, objectives are specialized, or interpretability matters (Borisov et al., 2022; Grinsztajn et al., 2022). Traditional feature engineering acknowledges this, but it is labor-intensive and domain-bound. Automated feature engineering partially addresses the search problem but usually relies on fixed transformation vocabularies. Genetic programming expands expressiveness, but often searches brittle tree spaces with limited computation reuse. Neural architecture search optimizes network form, but still assumes the main object to be trained is a neural model. We believe this is the wrong center of gravity. Machine learning should broaden toward a search-first perspective in which the primary learned object is a reusable computational structure. In this perspective, the main question is not only “which model should be trained?” but “which computations should exist?” Learning becomes the process of inventing, evaluating, recombining, and retaining useful computations. Once the right computations are discovered, prediction may require only a sparse linear combiner, a closed-form solver, a tiny readout layer, or a small number of trainable parameters. Heavy optimization becomes secondary rather than primary. This paper advances that position.
We propose EvoForest, a hybrid neuro-symbolic system that evolves a population-encoded directed acyclic graph of candidate computations. Each node contains multiple alternative implementations. Some nodes contain higher-order callable alternatives such as learnable activation families. A persistent @globals store holds trainable parameters that can be refined by gradient descent, while the graph structure itself is evolved by LLM-guided mutation. For each graph configuration, all output features are evaluated and linearly combined by a ridge classifier. The internals of that ridge model—importance, redundancy, residual structure, stability, and more—are converted into structured diagnostics and then fed back into the search process. Across multiple islands, the system accumulates strategic memory in the form of experiment memoranda that distill what is working, what is failing, and where search should move next. The details of EvoForest matter, but the larger point matters more. EvoForest is not just a feature-engineering method. It is evidence for a broader thesis: many machine learning problems can be approached more naturally by evolving computations. We do not argue that gradient-based learning should be abandoned. On the contrary, gradient descent remains one of the most powerful tools in the field. Our claim is that it should no longer monopolize the concept of learning. In many tasks, gradients are best used to refine a small number of continuous parameters inside a broader searched computational structure. In others, they may be unnecessary altogether. The deeper shift is conceptual: machine learning should be understood not only as parameter optimization, but as structured search over computations.
2. Position and Claims
This paper advances five main claims.
Claim 1: Machine learning has over-identified learning with parameter fitting
The field’s dominant abstraction treats intelligence as the result of optimizing large weight spaces. This has obscured a different but equally important source of learning: discovering the right computations. In many structured problems, the decisive step is not finding better parameters for a fixed architecture, but finding better transformations, tests, gates, summaries, and interaction structures.
Claim 2: Learning can be reframed as structured search over reusable computations
We propose that a broad class of ML tasks can be approached by explicitly searching for computational structures rather than relying on their implicit emergence inside large models. The main learned object becomes a reusable graph of candidate computations, not merely a weight tensor. This makes representational discovery explicit, controllable, and inspectable.
Claim 3: Parameter-light or closed-form predictors often suffice once the right computations are available
If the computational basis is expressive enough, downstream prediction does not always require a large trainable model. Sparse linear selection, ridge regression, or other minimal-parameter combiners can often extract most of the predictive value. This shifts effort away from heavy end-to-end fitting and toward computation discovery.
Claim 4: LLMs are natural search operators for computational hypothesis spaces
LLMs encode broad mathematical, algorithmic, and domain knowledge. Their most important role in this setting is not direct prediction, but guided invention: proposing transformations, combining motifs, repairing invalid candidates, and interpreting diagnostic feedback. They are search engines over computational hypotheses.
Claim 5: Hybrid neuro-symbolic systems should separate structural search from continuous refinement
One of the most promising directions is not purely symbolic search, nor purely neural training, but a principled separation of concerns: symbolic evolution determines what to compute; callable function families determine how signals may be transformed; sparse evaluators determine what matters; and gradient-based optimization refines only the small continuous subspaces that benefit from it. This separation is central to systems like EvoForest and, we argue, should become a broader research pattern.


3. Why the Field Needs a Search-First Paradigm
3.1 The limits of gradient-first thinking
Gradient descent is powerful when the objective is differentiable, the architecture is expressive enough, optimization is tractable, and the data are sufficient. But many practically important settings violate one or more of these assumptions. The target metric may be non-differentiable or poorly approximated by common surrogates. Data may be limited or highly structured. Interpretability may be essential. Domain priors may matter strongly. Small but meaningful invariants may be easier to encode symbolically than to recover from raw optimization. In such settings, scaling model size is often a poor substitute for discovering the right computations.
3.2 What feature engineering gets right—and wrong
Feature engineering persists because it recognizes that predictive performance often depends on the right transformations. It is the field’s recurring implicit admission that representation is not always best left to optimization. But feature engineering remains unsatisfactory as a general paradigm because it is manual, fragmented, and hard to scale. 
Search-first machine learning preserves feature engineering’s insight while generalizing its mechanism. Instead of asking humans to manually invent transformations, it uses programmatic search over compositional computational graphs. Instead of fixed feature libraries, it admits open-ended function invention. Instead of flat lists, it supports reusable intermediates. Instead of one-off feature scoring, it closes the loop through diagnostics and memory.
3.3 Why existing alternatives are incomplete
Genetic programming already explores symbolic computations, but typically through expression trees with fixed operator vocabularies and weak computation sharing. Automated feature engineering systems apply broad transformation templates, but rarely invent new classes of computation. Neural architecture search optimizes architectural structure, but not the broader open-ended space of arbitrary task-specific computations. Random feature methods show that simple linear models can be powerful on rich nonlinear feature spaces, but their features are not task-discovered. Mixture-of-experts assumes a fixed set of experts and learns a gate; it does not search for the expert computations themselves. Recent LLM-driven feature generation adds creativity, but usually lacks graph structure, strategic memory, continuous refinement, and diagnostic guidance. Search-first ML is meant to integrate the strengths of these traditions while removing their central bottleneck: the absence of a reusable, evolvable, diagnostically grounded computation space.
3.4 Why now
This paradigm is newly plausible because three technological conditions have changed. First, LLMs can now propose sophisticated domain-aware computations in executable form. Second, modern tensor systems and GPUs make large batches of candidate computations tractable, especially when graph structure enables sharing. Third, the limitations of pure parameter-centric learning are increasingly visible in structured domains where data, compute, or interpretability constraints matter. The ingredients for search-first ML now exist at practical scale.
4. EvoForest as an Exemplar of Search-First ML
EvoForest is best understood not as a finished destination, but as a proof of plausibility for the broader thesis.
4.1 A population-encoded graph of computations
A EvoForest is a directed acyclic graph in which each node contains multiple alternative implementations. Intermediate nodes represent competing computations from which a configuration selects one per node. Output-node alternatives are not competitors in the same way; they are features that are all evaluated for a given configuration and then scored jointly by a ridge classifier. This separates structural search from feature combination.
This representation inherits the core insight of EvoLattice: a population can be embedded inside a single graph whose alternatives coexist persistently. But EvoForest extends this into feature synthesis and hybrid optimization.
4.2 Reusable computation as a first-class object
Unlike flat feature-generation systems, EvoForest allows useful intermediate computations to be shared across many downstream features. Covariance matrices, spectral summaries, local statistics, trend measures, or learned transforms can be computed once and reused. This changes the economics of search. Breadth becomes tractable because reuse prevents redundant evaluation.
4.3 Higher-order callable search
A central innovation is the @-callable node. These nodes do not return tensors directly. They return functions. Downstream nodes receive these functions and invoke them. This turns function families—such as KAN, RBF, or gated activations—into explicit search objects. Structural search then factors into:
* which intermediate computations to compose,
* which callable families to use,
* which outputs to keep,
* and which continuous parameter values to refine.
This is a qualitatively different abstraction from both flat feature lists and conventional neural architecture search.
4.4 Persistent gradient-trained parameters
The @globals store contains persistent trainable parameters that survive across evolutionary steps. The LLM can add new parameters but does not rewrite existing trained values. Backpropagation refines parameter values within the currently best structural configuration. Thus structural evolution and continuous optimization are decoupled but cooperative. The LLM proposes where learnable flexibility should live; gradient descent determines the best values for that flexibility.
4.5 Evaluation through lightweight predictors with rich diagnostics
For each configuration, all output features are evaluated and scored using ridge regression with stratified cross-validation. Ridge is important here not only because it is fast, but because it is transparent. Since the model is linear and solved in closed form, its internals can be turned into diagnostics: feature importance, sign, pairwise redundancy, redundancy clusters, Spearman correlations, Cohen’s d, exact linear SHAP values, residual-nonlinearity signals, and cross-fold stability. This is not just evaluation. It is epistemic feedback. The model explains to the search system why certain features matter, where redundancy is accumulating, and where nonlinear structure remains unmodeled.
4.6 Strategic interpreted memory
EvoForest compresses diagnostics to a tabular representation optimized for LLM reasoning, and then further compresses the multi-step trajectory of those diagnostics into a persistent experiment memorandum for each island. The memorandum is not a replay buffer. It is a strategic interpretation of the search history: what classes of mutation helped, which features became central, which regions are underexplored, where redundancy is excessive, and which kinds of nonlinear transformations keep surfacing. This adds something that most automated search systems lack: interpreted memory.
4.7 LLMs as scientific search operators
In EvoForest, the LLM is not the classifier. It is closer to a scientist-engineer pair. One phase proposes hypotheses; another phase synthesizes concrete mutations. The LLM reasons over diagnostics, strategic memory, graph structure, and task context. It invents, recombines, repairs, prunes, and specializes computations. This is a compelling template for how LLMs can contribute to machine learning beyond acting as direct predictors.

5. Preliminary Evidence from Structural Regime Detection
In a preliminary experiment on structural regime detection in time series, the system operated on a dataset of 10,000 sequences and improved AUC from 0.600 to 0.749 over roughly 317 evolutionary steps in about 93 minutes on four GPUs. Across this run, it recorded 31 successive global-best improvements.
Several qualitative phenomena are more important than the raw score.
5.1 Autonomous rediscovery of classical statistical machinery
Without being explicitly told which classical methods to use, the system synthesized computations corresponding to:
* Gaussian KL divergence between pre- and post-break segments,
* CUSUM-style change-point statistics,
* spectral centroid and spectral entropy differences,
* higher-order autocorrelation structures,
* skewness and kurtosis differences,
* multi-scale variance ratios,
* boundary discontinuity measures normalized by local robust scale.
This matters because it suggests that LLM-guided search over computational graphs can rediscover domain-relevant statistical structure from task descriptions and tensor inventories.
5.2 Emergent hierarchical composition
The most powerful features were not isolated handcrafted summaries. They were deep compositions in which segment statistics fed nonlinear activations, which fed learnable gates, which then modulated higher-level feature interactions. The final best configuration used only 12 output features, down from 20 at the midpoint, while performance continued to improve. A single deeply composed gated feature accounted for roughly 74% of ridge importance. 
This is especially significant. It indicates that search-first systems need not simply accumulate broader feature banks. They can discover that fewer, deeper, better-composed features outperform many shallow ones.
5.3 Simplification-through-depth
One of the most striking phenomena in the preliminary run was the graph’s tendency to simplify itself as search matured. Early phases broadened the graph, inventing many new feature families. Later phases pruned aggressively while increasing internal compositional structure. The system moved from breadth-first discovery to depth-first refinement. This runs counter to the common intuition that automated feature systems should grow monotonically. Instead, EvoForest suggests that a diagnostically guided search process can learn when expressive depth substitutes for representational breadth.
5.4 Hybrid neuro-symbolic cooperation
The run also demonstrated that symbolic invention and continuous optimization can occupy distinct but complementary roles. The LLM introduced new callable families and new structural compositions. Gradient descent refined knot positions, kernel weights, gate scales, and projection matrices. Ridge then judged the resulting features using an interpretable downstream criterion. This division of labor is exactly the kind of separation of concerns we believe should characterize next-generation hybrid ML systems.
6. Relation to Existing Paradigms
6.1 Genetic programming
GP evolves symbolic expressions as predictive features (Koza, 1992; Espejo et al., 2010; La Cava et al., 2019). Search-first ML shares the basic instinct that symbolic or semi-symbolic search matters. But EvoForest-style systems differ in four important ways: reusable shared DAG structure, open-ended LLM-proposed function vocabularies, persistent memory, and optional continuous parameter refinement.
6.2 Neural architecture search
NAS evolves network structures and then trains them (Zoph & Le, 2017; Real et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2019). Search-first ML is broader. It does not just search network topology. It searches the computations themselves: statistics, transforms, tests, kernels, gates, and callable families. In this sense it generalizes architecture search into computational search.
6.3 Random features and kernel perspectives
Random feature methods demonstrate that rich nonlinear basis functions plus simple linear models can be highly effective (Rahimi & Recht, 2007; Bach, 2017). Search-first ML replaces randomness with targeted discovery. The resulting systems can be viewed as task-adaptive, structured, reusable generalizations of random feature machines.
6.4 Mixture-of-experts
MoE models use multiple experts and a gating mechanism (Jacobs et al., 1991; Shazeer et al., 2017; Fedus et al., 2022). Search-first ML shares the idea of multiple computational specialists, but the specialists themselves are searched and refined, not fixed in advance. Moreover, sparse selection may be closed-form or minimally parameterized rather than learned by a large gating network.
6.5 LLM-driven feature engineering
Recent work uses LLMs to propose features or feature transformations (Zhang et al., 2024; Gong et al., 2024; Yuksel & Sawaf, 2025). EvoForest extends this line by replacing flat feature sets with evolving reusable graphs, introducing higher-order callable families, persistent trainable parameters, configuration-based evaluation, structured diagnostics, and strategic memory. This is not merely “more features.” It is a different abstraction for how search is organized.
6.6 Program synthesis and scientific discovery
FunSearch and related systems show that LLMs can evolve programs solving combinatorial or mathematical tasks (Romera-Paredes et al., 2024). Search-first ML brings this spirit into prediction. The learned object is no longer a single monolithic program, nor merely a set of weights, but a reusable computational basis whose outputs feed a lightweight predictor. This suggests a convergence between program synthesis and machine learning.
7. Why Search-First ML Matters
7.1 Interpretability
Search-first systems make their computations explicit. Features, gates, projections, similarity functions, and interaction terms are inspectable. This creates a much more transparent development loop than one centered on opaque latent representations.
7.2 Domain flexibility
Because computations are explicitly proposed rather than implicitly induced, domain knowledge can enter naturally. LLMs can draw on broad mathematical and scientific priors to suggest task-relevant structures that would be difficult to encode through architecture design alone.
7.3 Compatibility with specialized objectives
Non-differentiable and domain-specific objectives remain difficult for pure gradient-based systems. Search-first methods can optimize with respect to ranking metrics, robustness criteria, regime metrics, or risk-sensitive measures more directly.
7.4 Small-data and structured-data regimes
When data are limited but the domain has rich structure, discovering the right computations may be far more sample-efficient than fitting a large representation learner from scratch.
7.5 Better allocation of computation
Instead of devoting nearly all budget to large-scale parameter fitting, search-first systems devote more compute to representational discovery and less to downstream optimization. This may be a better allocation in many structured tasks.
8. Objections and Limitations
Search-first ML is not a universal replacement for deep end-to-end learning. In high-data, large-scale unstructured domains, heavily trained neural models may remain superior. Search itself can be combinatorially expensive. LLM-generated code can be brittle. Lightweight combiners may miss interactions not explicitly represented in the feature space. Hybrid systems raise nontrivial engineering questions about evaluation cost, search stability, and memory design. These are real issues. But they are research challenges, not refutations of the broader thesis. Importantly, large-scale parameter fitting also has severe costs: opacity, overparameterization, objective mismatch, and weak incorporation of explicit domain structure. The relevant comparison is not search against an idealized frictionless baseline, but search against the actual limitations of today’s ML practice.
9. Conclusion
We have argued for a complementary paradigm to parameter-centric machine learning: search-first machine learning, in which learning is performed primarily through structured search over reusable computations, while sparse, closed-form, or otherwise minimal-parameter predictors determine how those computations are used.
We presented EvoForest as a concrete exemplar of this paradigm. EvoForest evolves a population-encoded DAG of candidate computations, supports higher-order callable search, preserves persistent trainable parameters, exploits graph-level computational sharing, and uses ridge-based diagnostics plus strategic memoranda to guide LLM-driven search. Preliminary results show that such a system can autonomously rediscover classical statistical machinery, compose it into deeper structures, and simplify itself toward fewer but more powerful features.
The broader point is not that EvoForest is the final answer. It is that machine learning should no longer be reduced to parameter optimization alone. A wider view is possible—one in which useful computations are explicitly searched, reused, refined, and selected. In this view, LLMs become scientific search operators, symbolic and neural mechanisms cooperate rather than compete, and representation learning becomes an explicit process of computational invention.
Machine learning should not only train models. It should also discover computations.
